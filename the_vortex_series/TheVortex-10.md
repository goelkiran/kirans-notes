# The stack is losing ground — what do we call the engineer who remains?

*Kiran's Notes — The Vortex, Article 10 of 12*

---

For the last decade, “full-stack engineer” has been one of the most useful job titles in software.

It named something real: a person who could work across the entire vertical slice of a system — frontend, backend, database, deployment. No handoffs. No waiting. One person who could take a feature from idea to production.

The title worked because the stack was a coherent concept.

I think that concept is starting to dissolve.

---

## What the stack was built on

The stack was always a simplification.

Each layer of a software system is its own world of specialisation. A world-class database engineer and a world-class frontend engineer share almost no technical vocabulary. What made “full-stack” workable was that the layers were bounded enough that a generalist could operate competently across them.

That boundedness was a product of the era.

Technologies were standardised. The frontend was JavaScript. The backend was one of a handful of dominant languages. The database was relational or document-based. Knowing “the stack” meant knowing a manageable combination.

But as I argued earlier in the series, many of these constraints were not technical. They were human.

They existed because the reader of the code was human.

---

## What changes when the reader is not human

Agentic AI can move across languages without the cognitive cost that humans carry.

The preference for a unified stack — so that any engineer can read any part of the system — was always a coordination mechanism.

When the reader is an agent, that constraint weakens.

The best tool for each specific job becomes available again — without the overhead that forced us to standardise.

That does not eliminate complexity.

It shifts where it sits.

---

## The specialisations that remain

If the stack is losing ground, it does not mean engineering loses structure.

It means the structure moves up.

The work that agents are absorbing is largely mechanical — translating well-specified intent into working code.

The work that remains is different.

The engineer who understands the domain deeply enough to specify intent precisely.

The engineer who governs the behaviour of the agents — evaluating outputs, understanding failure modes, deciding when to trust and when to question.

The architect who designs for accountability — not just correctness, but the ability to explain, audit, and stand behind a system.

These are not new ideas.

But they are becoming more visible as the implementation layer becomes less human-bound.

---

## What do we call this role?

I have been trying to find the right name.

“Full-stack” is too tied to the stack itself.

“AI engineer” is too narrow — it suggests building AI systems, not building systems with AI.

“Prompt engineer” was never quite right.

A few possibilities I find interesting:

*Intent engineer* — someone whose primary contribution is precise articulation.

*Systems governor* — someone responsible for behaviour, accountability, and reliability at scale.

Or perhaps the title simplifies.

Perhaps “software engineer” returns to something broader than it became during the stack era — the person responsible for the outcome, regardless of which layer or which tool produced it.

---

## The engineer who remains

The engineer who remains is the one who adds value where the agent cannot.

Deep domain understanding.

Architectural judgement under uncertainty.

Accountability.

And the ability to ask the right questions before anything is built.

Those were always the highest-value skills.

The vortex is simply making that visible.
