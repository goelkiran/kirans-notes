# The stack is losing ground — what do we call the engineer who remains?

For the last decade, "full-stack engineer" has been one of the most useful job titles in software. It named something real: a person who could work across the entire vertical slice of a system — frontend, backend, database, deployment. No handoffs. No waiting. One person who could take a feature from idea to production.

The title worked because the stack was a coherent concept. Frontend technologies, backend frameworks, database engines, cloud infrastructure — these were distinct layers with distinct skill sets, but a motivated engineer could learn all of them, and the industry rewarded those who did.

I think the stack is now dissolving. And with it, the usefulness of "full-stack" as a definition of engineering completeness.

---

## What the stack was built on

The stack, as a concept, was always a simplification. In reality, every layer of a software system is its own universe of specialisation. A world-class database administrator and a world-class frontend engineer share almost no technical vocabulary. What made "full-stack" workable was that the layers were each bounded enough that a generalist could operate competently across all of them without being expert in any.

That boundedness was a product of the era. Technologies were standardised. The frontend was JavaScript, the backend was one of three or four dominant languages, the database was a relational engine or perhaps a document store. Knowing "the stack" meant knowing a manageable set of technologies in a meaningful combination.

Now consider what is happening. Agentic AI can write competent code across any language without the cognitive cost that switching between languages imposes on a human. The preference for a unified stack — everyone using the same language so that anyone can read anyone else's code — was always a human coordination device. When the reader is an agent, that device is no longer necessary. The best tool for each specific job becomes available again, without the overhead.

The stack, as a concept, was a constraint we imposed on ourselves because of human cognitive limits. As those limits matter less, the concept weakens.

---

## The new specialisations that are emerging

If the stack is dissolving, what is replacing it? I do not think the answer is "no specialisation." I think the answer is that the specialisations are moving up the abstraction ladder.

The engineering work that agentic AI is absorbing is largely mechanical: translating a well-specified requirement into working code. This has always been the highest-volume, lowest-differentiation part of software engineering. It is the part that, if you are honest, many senior engineers found tedious.

What it is not absorbing — at least not yet, and perhaps not for a long time — is the work of understanding complex domains, making architectural trade-offs under uncertainty, governing systems at scale, and taking accountability for outcomes.

This suggests a new set of specialisations. Not "frontend engineer" or "backend engineer" — those are stack-defined roles. Something more like:

The engineer who understands the domain deeply enough to specify requirements that agents can execute correctly. In financial services, this is the person who knows that "position" means something very specific in investment operations and that getting it wrong has regulatory consequences. Domain-specific requirement precision is a skill that is becoming more valuable, not less.

The engineer who governs the agents themselves. Configuring, evaluating, auditing, and correcting the behaviour of agentic systems. Understanding the failure modes of different foundation models. Knowing when to trust an agent's output and when to be suspicious. This is a new specialisation that barely exists as a discipline today.

The architect who designs for governability. Not just technical correctness but accountability at scale — who is responsible for what, how decisions are audited, how the system behaves at the edges. This is the role I find myself thinking about most often.

---

## What to call the engineer who remains

I have been struggling to find the right name for the engineer of the near future. "Full-stack" is too tied to the stack concept. "AI engineer" is too focused on building AI systems rather than building systems with AI. "Prompt engineer" was always a placeholder that never quite captured the depth of the role.

A few possibilities I find genuinely interesting:

*Intent engineer* — someone whose primary contribution is the precise specification of intent, in a form that agents can execute reliably. The name captures the shift from implementation to articulation.

*Systems governor* — someone whose primary responsibility is the reliability, accountability, and auditability of systems that include AI participants. Less about writing code, more about ensuring the system does what it should across all conditions, with all the humans who depend on it.

Or perhaps the title simplifies rather than specialises. Perhaps "software engineer" returns to meaning something broader than it has in the stack era — the person who is responsible for the outcome of a software system, regardless of which layer of that system they touched or which tools they used to touch it.

The job title is a small thing. The underlying shift is not. The engineer who remains is the one who adds value that agents cannot yet provide: deep domain understanding, architectural judgement, accountability, and the ability to ask the right questions before any code is written.

Those skills have always been the most valuable ones. The vortex is simply making that more visible.
