# Hardware, software, and money — the vortex that built the digital economy

*Kiran's Notes — The Vortex, Article 2 of 12*

---

Before I can argue that something is changing, I need to explain what was built in the first place. To do that, I need to introduce a metaphor I will be using throughout this series.

I call it the vortex.

---

## Three characters, one spinning system

In the early days of computing, three characters dominated the story: processing power, storage, and connectivity. CPU, disk, and network. Each was expensive, slow, and scarce. And because they were scarce, every decision made around them was a decision about conservation.

You stored only what you had to. You processed only what you couldn’t avoid. You connected only what was physically nearby.

Then something interesting happened.

Each character got cheaper. Then faster. Then cheaper again.

And every time one of them improved, it created new possibilities for the others — which pulled investment, which drove further improvement, which created more possibilities.

This is the vortex.

Not a straight line of progress, but a system that compounds on itself. Each layer does not replace the previous one. It builds on it, reshapes it, and expands what becomes possible next.

Hardware enabled new software patterns. Those patterns enabled new ways of organising engineers. That scale of engineering required better connectivity. Connectivity made work global. Global work enabled offshoring. Offshoring expanded the engineering base. That base built for mobile. Mobile created always-on systems. Those systems drove cloud. Cloud made scale elastic. And GPUs accelerated everything again.

Each layer pulled the next into existence.

And the system kept spinning.

---

## The role money plays

There is another force in this system that is easy to miss.

Money.

Money is not separate from the vortex. It is both a product of it — and the fuel that keeps it spinning.

Each improvement in hardware or software creates economic value. That value attracts capital. That capital funds the next round of improvements. Which creates more value. Which attracts more capital.

This is why the vortex accelerates instead of stabilising.

It is not just a technical system.

It is an economic one.

---

## The economy the vortex built

Here is what the vortex produced — and it is worth pausing to appreciate the scale.

The five most valuable companies in the world today — Apple, Microsoft, NVIDIA, Alphabet, Amazon — are all fundamentally software and data businesses. They exist because the vortex made it possible to build at a scale that was previously unimaginable.

The digital economy did not just create new companies. It created entirely new categories of activity.

E-commerce is not a faster catalogue store — it is a system made possible by real-time data, distributed logistics, and reliable infrastructure.

Uber is not a faster taxi — it is a real-time coordination system across millions of participants.

Streaming, cloud software, digital payments — none of these exist without the vortex compounding across decades.

---

## The offshore wave was part of the vortex too

Here is something we rarely say plainly: the globalisation of software engineering was also a product of the vortex.

As connectivity improved, the cost of coordination across geographies dropped.

This made it viable — and then necessary — to build global teams.

Lower costs. Expanding talent pools. Better networks.

Each of these reinforced the others.

I am part of that story. I started my career at Infosys, working on systems for Deutsche Bank and UBS from Bangalore. The work was real. The impact was real.

And the model worked because the vortex made it possible.

The result was a global engineering workforce — hundreds of thousands of engineers contributing to the same systems.

And once that happened, a new problem emerged:

How do you coordinate an army at that scale?

---

## The vortex never stops

The reason I find this history important is not nostalgia.

It is because the vortex does not move in phases.

It accumulates.

Nothing disappears. Each layer remains — but changes meaning as new layers are added on top.

The same system that gave us global engineering teams is now incorporating a new participant.

A fourth character.

And when something new enters a system like this, it does not reset it.

It changes how everything that already exists behaves.

That is the shift we are beginning to see.
